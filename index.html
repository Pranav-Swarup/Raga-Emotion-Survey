About this study

Beyond the Scale

What you just heard

The hypothesis

  • A raga is not just its scale. Two ragas can share the exact same notes and still feel completely different, because of how those notes are approached, ornamented, and emphasised.
  • This study tests that directly: do Abheri and Reethigowla — two janya ragas of the same Kharaharapriya melakarta — actually make listeners feel different things?
  • If they do, a raga's emotional identity can't be reduced to its scale alone — it lives in the raga's characteristic treatment of the notes.

Method, briefly

  • A familiarisation clip (Brindavani, also a Kharaharapriya janya) comes first, to settle you into the instrument's timbre and the melakarta's sound before any rating — reducing timbre and scale as sources of bias.
  • The order the two rated clips play in is randomised per listener, and a short pause sits between them, so one clip's mood doesn't carry into the next rating.
  • Emotional response is measured with GEMS-9 (the Geneva Emotional Music Scale) — a validated nine-dimension scale from music-psychology research — rather than an ad-hoc list of moods.
  • All three clips are matched instrumental solo-violin renditions by the same performer, so the raga is the variable being tested, not the instrument or the artist.

What is a melakarta? What is a janya raga?

  • A melakarta is one of the 72 parent scales of Carnatic music — a fixed set of seven notes, nothing more. It has no built-in mood of its own.
  • A janya raga is "derived" from a melakarta: it can use the same notes (or a subset), but adds its own characteristic phrases, gamakas (ornamentation), emphasised notes, and notes it deliberately avoids. That treatment is where a raga's identity, and its feeling, actually lives — not in the bare list of notes.
  • So judging a raga's emotional character by its scale alone is a bit like judging a language by its alphabet: two ragas from one melakarta can be built from the same seven notes and still say very different things.
