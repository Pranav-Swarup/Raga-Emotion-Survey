About this study

Beyond the Scale

What you just heard

The Hypothesis

  • A raga is not just its scale. Two ragas can share the exact same notes and still feel emotionally different, because of how those notes are approached, the phrases characteristic to each raga, ornamentions, and emphasis on certain notes.
  • This study tests if Abheri and Reethigowla, two janya (derived/child) ragas of the same Kharaharapriya melakarta, actually make listeners feel different things?
  • If they do, a raga's emotional identity can't be reduced to its scale alone. It lives in the raga's characteristic treatment of the notes.

Method

  • A familiarisation clip set in the Brindavani Raga, also a Kharaharapriya janya comes first, to settle you into the instrument's timbre and the melakarta's sound before any rating, reducing timbre and scale as sources of bias.
  • The order the two rated clips play in is randomised per listener, and a short pause sits between them, so one clip's mood doesn't carry into the next rating.
  • Emotional response is measured with GEMS-9 (the Geneva Emotional Music Scale), a validated nine-dimension scale from music-psychology research.
  • All three clips are matched instrumental solo-violin renditions with a constant sruti (pitch) of D#, identical tempo of the manodharma (freestyle raga exploration), by the same performer Smt. Lalgudi Vijayalakshmi, so the raga is the only variable being tested.

What is a Melakarta? What is a janya raga?

  • A melakarta is one of the 72 parent scales of Carnatic music.
  • A janya raga is "derived" from a melakarta: it can use the same notes (or a subset), but adds its own characteristic phrases, gamakas (ornamentation), emphasised notes, and notes it avoids. That treatment is where a raga's identity, and its feeling lives.
  • So judging a raga's emotional character by its scale alone is a bit like judging a language by its alphabet: two ragas from one melakarta can be built from the same seven notes and still say very different things.
